PetBot — Technical Specification

An autonomous mobile robot that plays with a cat like interactive prey, and learns how your cat likes to play.

PetBot is a small, overhead-camera-instrumented, differential-drive "puck" wearing a soft feather lure. An ESP32 drives the wheels; a Mac (later, the cloud) is the ML brain. Rather than replaying a fixed library of human-authored moves, PetBot treats motion itself as the prey and learns the individual cat from how that cat responds. The deliverable is not the robot — it is the closed learning loop:

robot acts → cat responds → response is measured → data → policy improves → redeploy.

How to read this. This describes the system as designed and built, in present tense. The full software loop runs end to end — perception (AprilTag pose + pixel→floor homography + cat tracking), the world-state builder, the UDP brain↔︎robot link, and the on-robot firmware (physics-first MotionCommand executor, encoder PID, IMU, on-device safety envelope), with a robot emulator that closes the whole Mac↔︎robot loop in a self-test. The policy is trained by PPO inside a research-grounded cat-behavior simulator (§3.4) and then personalizes to the individual cat through brief on-robot fine-tuning. Every architectural seam is a verified contract in brain/contracts/.

1. Overview

Cats are ambush predators wired to chase motion patterns, not shapes. A feather that darts, freezes, twitches, and flees the instant it is watched is prey; the same feather sitting still is furniture. PetBot's core idea follows directly: the trajectory is the bait. The robot's job is to produce physical motion — a sequence of velocities and accelerations — that a specific cat finds worth chasing, and to keep finding better motion as it learns that cat.

This produces three explicit project goals, in priority order:

  1. Learn ML infrastructure / ML-systems engineering. The primary goal. PetBot is an end-to-end MLOps sandbox (§8): data ingestion, time-synchronization, dataset versioning, feature stores, experiment tracking, a model registry, offline evaluation gates, drift monitoring, and staged OTA deployment — all owned by the builder, at a scale small enough to understand every stage.
  2. Learn robotics. The full autonomy stack in miniature — perception → localization → state estimation → prediction → policy → control — the same backbone as a self-driving car, scaled to a living room.
  3. Build a genuinely cool adaptive product. A creature that measurably learns your cat, with a credible path from one robot to a multi-home fleet.

Everything in this spec serves the loop. The robot acts (§5), the cat responds and is measured (§4, §6), the measurements become versioned data (§8), a policy improves (§3, §7), and the improved policy is redeployed to the fleet.

PetBot implements the canonical autonomy stack, split cleanly across two compute domains and four durable software contracts.

2.1 Perception and the two cameras

Overhead "privileged" camera (in the loop). A ceiling/corner-mounted webcam (Logitech C920x, 1080p @ 30 fps) looks down on a bounded play area. This camera is privileged: it sees the whole scene from above and produces near-ground-truth measurement.

  • Robot pose comes from an AprilTag (ArUco 36h11) mounted flat on the robot's top deck — an unambiguous, low-jitter position and heading every frame.
  • Arena calibration is a per-placement homography mapping image pixels to metric floor coordinates (meters), plus fixed anchor tags at known floor positions so a bumped camera re-solves its own pose and rebuilds the homography automatically.
  • Cat tracking (YOLO on the Mac's GPU) yields the cat's floor position; velocity, acceleration, heading, and gaze are derived downstream.

Onboard camera (egocentric, not yet in the loop). A forward-facing ESP32-CAM streams egocentric video. In V0 it is a passive logger. Its long-term role is privileged supervision: the overhead camera's exact positions automatically label the onboard camera's frames, generating — for free, during normal play — a large egocentric dataset with which onboard perception is later trained to reproduce the overhead ground truth. This is what eventually lets the robot leave the overhead camera behind (V3).

2.2 Two compute domains

Domain Hardware Runs Rate
Robot ESP32 (+ TB6612 driver, MPU-6050 IMU, wheel encoders) Motion executor, PID, odometry, safety envelope 100+ Hz
Brain Mac (later cloud) Perception, world-state, policy, logging, training 2–5 Hz (control)

Decoupled control is why a cheap puck feels crisp. The Mac's policy decides which motion to make at 2–5 Hz over Wi-Fi; the ESP32 executes that motion at 100+ Hz using local encoder + IMU feedback and never waits on the network. Commands and telemetry ride UDP (low latency, loss-tolerant); at session start a round-trip ClockSync handshake lands every stream on one common clock.

2.3 Safety envelope — below the policy

The learned policy never gets unrestricted actuator authority. A deterministic safety envelope runs on the ESP32, beneath the policy: hard caps on linear/angular speed and acceleration, a PWM ceiling, and a watchdog that brakes the motors if commands stop arriving. Safety is independent of the Mac and independent of the learning; a buggy or adversarial policy output is clamped in hardware before it can reach a motor.

2.4 The four contracts

The system is organized around four seams (brain/contracts/). Getting these clean makes every future upgrade — onboard perception, a second robot, cloud training, OTA — a localized swap rather than a rewrite.

Contract Seam Guarantee
world_state perception ↔︎ policy The policy never sees pixels — only a WorldState of measurable physics. Makes the camera swap local and cross-device data poolable.
messages brain ↔︎ robot Physics-first MotionCommand down, Telemetry up, over UDP.
session system ↔︎ store Multi-stream, time-aligned, versioned episodes with fleet-ready keys.
policy learning ↔︎ serving Swappable, versioned policy/dynamics artifacts (enables registry + OTA).

3. From research to policy

The learning pipeline runs in four stages, in order: (1) encode the ethology research into the moves that engage a cat and the signal that rewards them, (2) build an RL environment around a research-grounded cat, (3) grow a world model for sample-efficient, safe training, and (4) train the policy with PPO — which rediscovers those moves from continuous control alone.

3.1 Pre-RL — the moves research says make a cat play

Before any learning, the question is what should the robot's motion actually do? Decades of feline-behavior research answer it. These are the moves that measurably spike engagement — each grounded, not guessed:

Move What the robot does Why it works (research)
Motion is the prey move at all; a still robot is furniture Object play is triggered by movement, small size, and texture — not shape. (Hall & Bradshaw, Object play in adult domestic cats)
Flee on contact / attention dart away the instant the cat locks on or lunges Fleeing prey releases the chase; prey that approaches the predator does not.
Gaze-contingent darting move when the cat looks away, freeze when it's watched Cats orient to motion onset; darting in the blind moment and freezing under direct gaze mimics real prey.
Freeze-then-burst hold still, then a sudden acceleration A sudden move after stillness triggers the pounce far more than steady motion.
Protean (unpredictable) escape vary direction and speed unpredictably Predators counter-adapt to predictable prey; unpredictable escape sustains pursuit. (responsive-robotic-prey studies; optimal-vs-protean escape models)
Never repeat for long keep changing the trajectory Interest habituates without change and is renewed by novelty. (Hall & Bradshaw change-detection)
Let the cat win — sometimes allow the occasional capture, then re-engage Never winning breeds frustration; always winning breeds boredom; sustained play is a per-cat balance.
Small, low, ground-hugging stay a small, low, fast target Prey-like size and profile; a low puck reads as prey, not a threat.

None of these are hard-coded as robot behaviors. They define what to reward (§6) and how the simulated cat reacts (§3.2). The policy rediscovers them on its own from continuous physical control — we don't program "flee," we reward engagement and watch fleeing emerge (§3.4).

3.2 The RL environment — a research-grounded cat

The moves above are compiled into a parametric cat that turns the priors into a trainable environment. It is not a naive puppet — it is a research-grounded model of feline predatory play, and it is where the policy learns what "prey-like motion" means before it ever meets a cat.

The cat model. A parametric cat runs an internal predatory-sequence state machine — orient → stalk → chase → pounce → capture → recover — gated by two hidden drives grounded in the ethology literature:

  • Prey drive is triggered by motion, not shape (Hall & Bradshaw, Object play in adult domestic cats): a small, moving, erratic stimulus is prey; the same object sitting still is furniture. In simulation a motionless robot draws essentially zero interest — the policy must move to engage.
  • Habituation + change-detection: interest is high at first, decays under repetition, and is renewed by novelty. This is the single most important dynamic, because it is precisely why a good policy must keep varying its motion.
  • Protean pursuit–evasion: the cat chases the robot's lagged position under its own speed/acceleration limits, so sharp, unpredictable maneuvers make it overshoot — evasion emerges from physics rather than being scripted (cf. responsive-robotic-prey studies of how predators adapt to predictable escape).

What the simulator demonstrates. The engagement signal behaves exactly as the ethology predicts (representative sessions from the simulator's self-test):

Robot motion Cat engagement Habituation
dead-still 0.00 —
random / directionless ≈ 0.31 low
varied, prey-like (flee + protean jitter) ≈ 0.60 low — sustained
a repetitive constant circle ≈ 0.02 saturates — the cat tunes it out

A policy that drives a lazy, predictable circle is bored away within seconds; one that flees, feints, and keeps varying its motion holds attention. That is the training pressure PPO optimizes against — and it rediscovers prey-like behavior from continuous control alone, with no behavior menu.

3.3 The world model — sample-efficient, safe training

Running millions of live cat interactions is impossible, so PPO trains in a model, not on the animal. Two models compound: the research-grounded cat prior (§3.2) gives a warm start with zero real data, and a learned dynamics model — fit from real episodes to predict the next state (robot + short-horizon cat response) from history + action — sharpens the simulator as real play accumulates. Both run under domain randomization: every episode instantiates a different cat (chase threshold, speed, patience, habituation rate) with randomized command latency, wheel slip, and tracking noise, so the policy is robust in transfer. The simulator is explicitly a prior, not ground truth — the policy warm-starts here, then fine-tunes briefly on the real cat beneath the §2.3 safety envelope, and a sim-only policy is never shipped.

3.4 The policy — PPO

The action space is continuous physical control — target velocities over a short horizon — not a menu of named behaviors. There is no flee, feint, freeze-burst, or allow-capture in the vocabulary; those are emergent analysis labels. Hard-coding a behavior menu would foreclose the discovery the project exists to make.

PetBot is a POMDP — the true cat state (hunger, arousal, intent) is never directly observed. Observation o_t = the physics history window (§4); action a_t = a MotionCommand (§5); reward r_t = windowed engagement (§6); π_θ = actor, V_φ = critic.

The learner is Proximal Policy Optimization — on-policy actor-critic:

  • Clipped surrogate objective min(ρ_t·Â_t, clip(ρ_t, 1−ε, 1+ε)·Â_t), ρ_t = π_θ/π_θ_old, ε ≈ 0.2 — bounds each update so it can't take a destructive step.
  • GAE (λ ≈ 0.95, γ ≈ 0.99) trades bias vs. variance in Â_t.
  • Composite loss: clipped policy loss + value MSE + an entropy bonus for exploration.
  • ≈ 4–10 minibatch epochs per rollout (minibatches ≈ 64), reusing on-policy data before discarding it.
  • Gaussian continuous policy: the actor emits a state-dependent mean and a learned log-std per action dimension; actions are sampled and squashed into the normalized [−1, 1] range, then rescaled to physical (v, ω).

Network: actor + critic MLPs over the stacked observation — 2–3 hidden layers × 256, tanh, shared trunk, separate heads; low-dim physics, no pixels, so a small MLP suffices. Why PPO: stable, hyperparameter-forgiving, sample-efficient, natively continuous, and a proven sim-to-real workhorse — exactly PetBot's regime.

3.5 Global prior + per-cat personalization

A shared policy is trained across the whole fleet — many cats, homes, and robots — capturing what tends to engage cats in general. A new cat starts from this global prior and specializes quickly via lightweight per-cat adaptation (fine-tuning and/or context conditioning on a per-cat identity embedding). This is the data flywheel of §7: the fleet lifts the prior; each robot personalizes locally.

4. Observation / feature space

The observation is measurable physics only, drawn directly from WorldState (brain/contracts/world_state.py). There are no semantic mood labels (no cat_is_shy) — tendencies are learned implicitly from history. A single frame cannot tell "stalking" from "losing interest," so the policy consumes a stacked N-frame history window (N ≈ 8–16, ≈ 2–5 Hz).

Gaze (head_direction, attending_robot) is first-class: the emergent optimal move is often to dart when the cat looks away and freeze when it is watched. Context features are kept deliberately narrow — with scarce per-cat data, a firehose of features overfits; a small curated set turns hidden confounders (hunger, energy, time-of-day) into useful signal.

5. Action space

The learned action is a continuous physical MotionCommand (brain/contracts/messages.py):

Field Units Range Notes
v_mps m/s ≈ ±1.4 (capped) target linear velocity
omega_radps rad/s capped target angular velocity
duration_s s short horizon hold the setpoint
setpoints[] — optional short-horizon trajectory

The Mac emits a MotionCommand at 2–5 Hz; the on-robot PID controller tracks it at 100+ Hz using encoder + IMU feedback; the safety envelope (§2.3) clamps everything below that. There is no PWM at the learned interface and no named-behavior enum — embodiment is deliberately separated from behavior, so the same policy can outlive the current chassis.

Emergent behavior. All the recognizable "moves" — a darting flee, a hesitant feint, a freeze-then-burst, a peek-and-retreat around a couch — are windows of continuous control that PPO discovers because they earn engagement reward. Notably, "letting the cat win" is not a hardcoded action: it emerges as a slow, trailing trajectory precisely because the windowed reward (§6) values the future re-engagement a capture produces.

6. Reward — engagement

Reward is windowed / long-horizon and computed entirely from observable cat behavior — never from a guessed mood. It is defined on top of the recorded evidence, so it can evolve without touching the dataset.

Sign Signals
Positive (+) cat accelerates toward robot/toy, turns to follow, chases, paws, pounces, sustained orientation/attention, renewed pursuit after a pause, decreasing distance, longer interaction duration
Negative (−) leaves the play area, attention elsewhere, repeated retreat, inactivity, avoidance, stress signs

Safety events override reward unconditionally: fear/distress, collision, cornering, or unsafe contact trigger de-escalation and are never traded for a higher engagement score (§2.3).

The pipeline keeps four layers strictly separable (brain/contracts/session.py), so the reward definition can change without rewriting a single recorded episode:

  1. Raw observations — media streams, telemetry, issued commands.
  2. Derived features — velocities, accelerations, distances (pure physics).
  3. Engagement metrics — observable cat-behavior evidence (not yet a scalar).
  4. Reward function — defined on top of layers 1–3, versioned separately.

Because reward is windowed, the optimal capture rate emerges rather than being tuned by hand: never letting the cat win breeds frustration; always letting it win breeds boredom; the reward-maximizing policy finds the balance for this cat.

7. What it learns / how

PetBot learns a cat's play preferences implicitly from interaction history — nothing is hand-labeled. Different cats reward different physics, and the policy discovers each cat's tendencies:

  • lateral vs. head-on movement,
  • preferred approach speed and acceleration profiles,
  • the value of pauses, stillness, and sudden reversals,
  • aerial ("vertical prey") vs. ground-hugging motion,
  • novelty-seeking vs. reward from repetition.

The mechanism is the reward of §6 applied to the observations of §4: the cat's own motion is the training label (self-supervised), so the environment hands over the signal for free during ordinary play. Across a fleet this becomes a data flywheel: pooled episodes train the global prior, and each robot's local episodes drive per-cat specialization — more homes make every robot better, and every robot still adapts to the animal in front of it.

8. ML infrastructure

This is the primary deliverable. PetBot is deliberately built as an end-to-end MLOps sandbox, and the builder learned ML-systems engineering by owning every stage of the loop rather than importing a platform. Walking the whole ladder by hand is the point: the pain of each missing capability at small scale is what makes the reason for each production tool concrete instead of cargo-culted.

Capability How PetBot exercises it
Multimodal ingestion + time-sync Overhead video, onboard video, audio, and 50–100 Hz telemetry are stamped to one common clock via a ClockSync handshake and per-stream sync offsets. This synchronization is the genuinely hard data-engineering problem.
Episode / data model A session is an episode: a folder of multi-stream, time-aligned media + an append-only tick log + metadata, with fleet-ready keys robot_id / cat_id / play_area_id and version stamps from day one.
Dataset versioning Raw observations are separated from derived features (§6), so recomputing features never mutates raw data; both are versioned.
Feature store The stacked-history + curated-context feature view is computed once and served identically at train and serve time — closing train/serve skew.
Experiment tracking Every training run records hyperparameters, dataset version, and metrics for reproducibility.
Model registry Policies and dynamics models are swappable, versioned artifacts (the policy contract), stamped into each session's metadata (behavior_policy_version, vision_model_version).
Offline / off-policy evaluation New policies are scored against held-out logged episodes as a deploy gate before any robot runs them.
Distribution shift New cat, new home, new robot, or new toy shifts the input distribution; the global-prior-plus-personalization design (§3.5) and drift monitoring address it explicitly.
Closed-loop, non-IID data The robot's actions change the future data it collects — the data is not IID, which is exactly why PPO is on-policy and why the dynamics model + domain randomization matter.
Model / data / hardware lineage Each episode records firmware_version, hardware_version, and hardware_config — embodiment is logged alongside behavior so results stay attributable across chassis changes.
Staged / canary OTA Improved policies deploy to a canary subset of the fleet before a full rollout, with rollback.
Fleet telemetry + drift monitoring Aggregated per-cat/per-robot metrics (cat_timeline) surface engagement drift and hardware anomalies.
Federated learning (optional) A path to training on the global prior without centralizing raw home video.

9. Hardware

PetBot is a small differential-drive "puck": a laser-cut chassis, two encoder micro-gearmotors driven by an ESP32 (with an IMU and an on-device safety envelope), a soft breakaway feather lure, and a foam shell — plus an overhead webcam that gives the privileged, near-ground-truth view of the play area. Everything on the robot is off-the-shelf hobby electronics.

Building one is a weekend hardware project. The full itemized parts list (with links) and a step-by-step build guide (wiring, bring-up order, safety) live alongside this spec — see PARTS_LIST.md and BUILD_GUIDE.md.

10. Roadmap

The single-robot system — the robot, the overhead-camera scaffold, the full learning loop, and the cat-behavior simulator that warm-starts the policy — is built. The road from here is about scale, not the first build; the same loop runs the whole way, and each stage industrializes one more piece.

Stage Scope ML-infra lesson
Now Single robot + overhead-camera scaffold + PPO trained in the cat-behavior simulator; local logging + offline evaluation. The whole loop, by hand, on one machine — schema, provenance, sim-training, first models, offline eval.
Next Harden the single-device loop into a reproducible pipeline. Automated/reproducible training, experiment tracking, model registry, active-learning labeling.
Fleet Multiple cats/rooms and a second robot; centralized training. Centralized ingestion, aggregated dataset, global prior + per-cat training, staged/canary OTA.
Onboard Onboard-perception product; the overhead camera retires. Production ML platform: distributed ingestion, dataset/feature versioning, continuous training, registry + canary + rollback, drift monitoring, edge/OTA.
